## "The Man Who Outprinted the Bank of England: How a Garden Shed Nearly Broke British Currency"
### The Michael Faraday £20 Note, Operation Mermaid, and the Fifty Million Pound Forgery Ring

**Estimated runtime:** ~14–16 minutes  
**Format:** Narrated investigative documentary, cold analytical tension, verified facts only

Picture this: a twenty-pound note lands on the green felt of a London casino table. It is inspected under warm halogen lamps, counted, and stacked. By morning, it sits inside the vault of the Bank of England itself — verified, stamped, and officially cleared as genuine.

Except it isn't. It never was.

That note was born days earlier in a garden shed in Essex. It was pulled from the rolling cylinders of a secondhand printing press, guided by the ink-stained fingers of a soft-spoken grandfather with no criminal record and an obsessive mastery of paper chemistry.

For four years, that shed produced what Scotland Yard would later call the largest counterfeiting operation in British history. Tens of millions of pounds flooded the country — notes so convincing that the Bank of England's own automated sorting machines stamped them genuine.

This is the story of how two very different men nearly broke a three-hundred-year-old institution, and how the smallest clerical mistake brought the whole thing down.

It began with a hustler and a craftsman.

Steven Jory was an East London operator. Throughout the 1980s, he worked the black market selling counterfeit designer clothes and fake luxury perfumes from car boots and street stalls. His operation was crude: cheap chemical fragrances poured into generic glass bottles, finished with fake Chanel No. 5 labels slapped on in back rooms. When Customs and Excise shut him down, Jory walked away with an invaluable lesson. Knockoff perfume carried high risk for small margins. To make real fortunes, you did not counterfeit fifty-pound bottles. You counterfeited the money used to buy them.

Jory had the ambition and the street network to distribute illicit merchandise across Britain. What he lacked was the technical genius required to defeat the most heavily guarded banknote on Earth.

That missing piece arrived through family. By the early 1990s, Jory was dating the daughter of a man named Kenneth Mainstone.

To his neighbors in Essex, Mainstone was the picture of suburban respectability. A calm family man in his fifties, he was known as the quiet tradesman who always said hello over the fence. For more than twenty years, Mainstone had run a small commercial print shop in Essex. Business cards, leaflets, wedding invitations — nothing you would look at twice.

Yet behind that ordinary facade lay a master technician. Mainstone had spent decades studying paper chemistry, lithography, pigment suspension, and color separation. He understood how ink interacted with organic fibers at a microscopic level. Jory saw in him a rare skill set that could be worth millions.

It began over Sunday dinners. Casual conversation about printing drifted toward the theoretical vulnerabilities of British currency. Jory tested the waters with jokes about whether a small commercial press could ever replicate a real banknote.

Gradually, curiosity overcame caution. The challenge fascinated Mainstone as an intellectual puzzle. He had spent his adult life producing unappreciated stationery; the Bank of England was the ultimate Everest of printing.

Mainstone quietly moved a commercial offset litho press into an outbuilding behind his house in Essex. Blackout blinds were fitted to the windows, and ventilation was installed to disperse chemical solvents. A law-abiding family man became the technical brain behind an illicit national mint.

Their target was the Bank of England's Series D twenty-pound note, featuring the scientist Michael Faraday. Issued in 1991, Threadneedle Street considered it one of the most heavily defended pieces of paper on Earth. The Bank assumed that to forge it, a criminal ring would need to operate a private paper mill and a national mint simultaneously.

Four formidable security barriers stood in their way.

First was the paper. Legitimate banknotes are not made from standard commercial stock; they are crafted from custom cotton-linen rag that contains zero optical brighteners, staying dark under ultraviolet light while ordinary paper glows neon violet.

Second was intaglio printing. Massive steel plates pressed thick, textured ink into the paper under heavy pressure, creating raised ridges on Faraday's coat that anyone could feel with a thumb.

Third was the watermark. Faraday's likeness was woven directly into the wet paper fibers during manufacturing, revealing a soft, continuous-tone portrait when backlit.

Fourth was the metallic security thread. A silver ribbon was embedded inside the wet paper pulp during manufacturing, appearing on the surface as broken dashes that formed a solid line against the light.

For ordinary counterfeiters, these defenses were an absolute dead end. For Mainstone, they were engineering hurdles waiting to be solved.

Working in secret in his shed, Mainstone spent months experimenting. Through European merchants, he sourced specialty rag paper that absorbed UV light exactly like official banknote paper, treating it with sizing agents to replicate the distinct crisp snap of real tender.

To reproduce the intricate portrait and guilloche patterns, Mainstone shunned digital scanners, which produced tell-tale pixelation. Instead, he used traditional graphic reproduction cameras, breaking the Faraday note down into seventeen separate, hand-corrected color separation plates.

The intaglio feel was replicated through multi-pass printing using high-viscosity inks that dried with a tactile ridge. For the watermark, he formulated a translucent chemical solution applied between paper layers.

The metallic thread was the real obstacle. You cannot weave a thread into paper that has already been made. Mainstone improvised, printing each note in two passes, bonding a thin metallic foil strip directly into the surface while the ink was curing, then overprinting the broken white dashes. Held to the light, it looked identical to the real security thread. A bank, a mill, and a mint — all beaten from a garden shed.

Mainstone was obsessive about quality. For every note that left that shed, two or three imperfect sheets went straight into the fire out back. Nothing sloppy made it out the door.

While Mainstone perfected the product, Jory built the pipeline. Beginning in 1994, Jory established an underground wholesale network across South London, selling wholesale bricks of fake twenties to middlemen at twenty to thirty pence on the pound.

From there, the fake notes moved through the cash economy: bookmakers at dog tracks, West End casinos, crowded pubs, and petrol stations where cashiers rarely checked notes under UV lights.

By 1996, the shed operated at industrial capacity. Mainstone and Jory expanded to the Series E fifty-pound note. At their peak, investigators estimated two out of every three counterfeit notes in circulation across the UK came out of that one shed — around fifty million pounds in face value.

And here is the part that should terrify you: the Bank of England's own automated sorting machines, the ones meant to catch fakes before they went back into circulation, were stamping them genuine. The notes were bundled and recirculated back into bank branches and cash machines across the country.

The situation became so severe that inside Threadneedle Street, officials seriously debated pulling the entire Faraday note out of circulation. It would have cost tens of millions of pounds and served as an embarrassing public admission that their currency had failed.

Instead, authorities launched Operation Mermaid — a covert task force uniting Scotland Yard's Organised Crime Group, forensic paper chemists, and Bank of England investigators to hunt down the source.

Scotland Yard quickly realized chasing the cash itself was pointless. It moved like water, with no names attached and no trail to follow. Detectives realized their only option was to trace the supply chain. Someone in Britain was buying specific supplies: non-brightened paper, specialty inks, and hot-foil stamping equipment.

Jory's crew had been careful, using fake names, front corporate addresses, burner pagers, and cash wherever possible. But in high-level industrial procurement, automated bureaucracy rarely forgets.

The breakthrough came from an absurdly routine clerical error.

A Midlands equipment supplier, from whom Jory's team made an early purchase, added the buyer's account to an automated marketing mailing list. Months after the operation moved locations, glossy trade sales catalogues kept arriving at the old, deserted warehouse.

New tenants moved in, found a pile of junk mail addressed to strangers, and threw it straight into the trash. A Scotland Yard detective, sifting through commercial waste as part of the investigation, pulled one of those catalogues back out.

On the mailing label was a customer reference number.

Traced back through the supplier's records, it led detectives to a single credit card payment made before the gang switched entirely to cash. Around the same time, investigators intercepted a bundle of counterfeit notes in South London with something tucked inside: a promotional flyer for Mainstone's print shop, complete with a handwritten pager number for Steven Jory.

That was it. That was the whole case.

On August 14th, 1998, the raids came all at once. Police tactical units struck an associate's base on the Isle of Wight, recovering one and a half million pounds in uncut sheets. At the exact same time, officers breached the garden shed in Essex, finding the press, the plates, chemical baths, and Mainstone's hand-cut color separations still sitting on the workbenches.

In court, the conspirators diverged. Jory and his network pleaded guilty and cooperated, earning Jory an eight-year sentence.

Mainstone fought it, insisting in court that he genuinely believed his press was only being used to print glossy magazines and novelty props. The jury did not believe a word of it. As the technical mind behind the entire fifty-million-pound operation, Kenneth Mainstone was sentenced to twelve years in prison — four years more than the man who recruited him.

The Bank of England was forced to rush out a completely redesigned twenty-pound note featuring Edward Elgar with holographic foils, costing the British economy over one hundred million pounds.

Two men in a garden shed had done what almost nobody thought was possible: they had made a three-hundred-year-old central bank rewrite its own money. And in the end, the craftsman who had spent a lifetime mastering every kind of paper was finally caught by the one piece of paper he never touched — a piece of junk mail nobody was even supposed to read.
