### [1:20 – 3:00] CHAPTER 1 — THE PERFECT BANK

Danske Bank wasn't some shady offshore operation. Founded in 1871, it was Denmark's largest and most respected financial institution. Safe. Boring. Trustworthy.

In 2007, Danske Bank acquired a small Finnish bank called Sampo Bank. Buried inside was something nobody paid attention to: a tiny branch in Tallinn, the capital of Estonia.

On paper, that branch was nothing. A handful of employees. Not even a rounding error on Danske Bank's balance sheet.

But that branch had one unusual feature. It served a "non-resident portfolio" — accounts belonging to foreign customers, mostly from Russia and former Soviet states, who didn't live in Estonia. Shell companies with vague names, registered in places like the UK, moving enormous sums of money in and out for reasons no one questioned.

It looked like profit. The branch was tiny, but punching far above its weight, generating a huge share of the bank's earnings. Nobody wanted to ask why.

### [3:00 – 5:30] CHAPTER 2 — THE BRANCH WITH A SECRET

The sheer scale didn't match the operation. Estonia has a population smaller than many cities. Its entire economy is a fraction of the money passing through this one branch.

Between 2007 and 2015, roughly two hundred billion euros — later estimated at up to two hundred and thirty billion dollars — flowed through that single branch.

Warning signs were obvious. The same amounts moving in circles between shell companies. Payments with no business explanation. Customers whose declared activity had nothing to do with the cash volume.

Compliance staff flagged concerns for years. Regulators sent warnings. But in Copenhagen, the message never landed with urgency. Profit kept arriving. Questions kept getting deferred. And the people who might have stopped it were either not listening — or not being listened to.

That's where one man comes in.

### [5:30 – 7:45] CHAPTER 3 — THE WHISTLEBLOWER

His name is Howard Wilkinson. A British banker inside Danske Bank's Baltic operations, with direct visibility into Estonia.

Wilkinson raised the alarm internally, repeatedly, starting years before the world heard "Danske Bank scandal." He warned senior management about suspicious accounts, about money that made no sense.

For years, very little changed.

Eventually, Berlingske began publishing investigative articles in 2017, laying out how much suspicious money had moved through the branch and how long warnings had gone unheeded.

When Wilkinson's identity became public, his life changed instantly. He'd exposed a case involving hundreds of billions in suspected dirty money — tied to organized crime and figures close to the Russian state.

Being named in a case touching Russian money on this scale stays with you. One man, doing his job honestly, ends up permanently looking over his shoulder — because the money was connected to people who do not like being exposed.

### [8:15 – 10:15] CHAPTER 4 — THE MAGNITSKY CONNECTION

This stops being just a banking story and becomes a geopolitical thriller.

Investigators tracing the dirty money found links to the "Russian Laundromat" — a network moving illicit funds out of Russia and into the Western financial system.

Some threads led directly to the death of Sergei Magnitsky — one of the most infamous cases in modern Russian history.

Magnitsky was a Russian tax lawyer who uncovered a massive fraud involving Russian officials stealing hundreds of millions from the state. He exposed it. He was arrested. In 2009, he died in a Moscow prison under circumstances described as serious mistreatment.

Bill Browder — the investor who employed Magnitsky — later filed a legal case alleging Danske Bank's Estonian branch channeled funds connected to the fraud.

Reporting also pointed to alleged links between suspicious transactions and senior Russian government figures, though who benefited from all two hundred and thirty billion has never been mapped.

This wasn't just careless banking. It was one small branch acting as a pipe connecting Russian dirty money to the entire Western financial system.

### [10:15 – 11:45] CHAPTER 5 — THE UNRAVELING

Once Berlingske's reporting went public, the dominoes fell fast.

Danske Bank's internal investigation in September 2018 confirmed regulators' fears: the vast majority of transactions were suspicious.

CEO Thomas Borgen resigned. He would later face charges.

Estonia's regulator ordered Danske Bank shut down. Investigations opened in France. The DOJ and SEC launched probes. Deutsche Bank's Frankfurt headquarters was raided over its role processing suspicious payments.

One branch's dirty secret had become one of the largest financial crime investigations in European history.

### [11:45 – 13:00] CHAPTER 6 — THE DEATH IN TALLINN

In September 2019, this turned from a financial scandal into something the entire world was paying attention to.

Aivar Rehe had run Danske Bank's Estonian branch from 2007 to 2015 — the exact years the laundering took place. He wasn't a suspect.

On a Monday morning, he left his home and didn't come back. Police searched two days. They found his body near his house. No signs of violence. Ruled a suicide.

This was a real person, and a real tragedy for his family. But it shows how heavy the weight of this case became for the people closest to it.

### [13:00 – 14:15] CHAPTER 7 — THE RECKONING

That final bill came. In December 2022, Danske Bank pleaded guilty in a U.S. federal court to conspiracy to commit bank fraud — admitting it had lied to American banks about its anti-money laundering controls in Estonia.

Danske Bank agreed to forfeit and pay more than two billion dollars. One of the largest money laundering penalties ever.

But the full truth of where all two hundred and thirty billion dollars actually ended up may never be known.
