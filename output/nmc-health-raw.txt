In the mid-1970s, a young pharmacist named Bavaguthu Raghuram Shetty arrived in Abu Dhabi from a small town in southern India, reportedly with only a few dollars to his name. No connections. No capital. No family wealth to fall back on. Just a pharmacy diploma and a plan to open a single medical clinic in a city that barely had a private healthcare sector worth mentioning.
Four decades later, that single clinic had become NMC Health, a hospital network treating four million patients a year across nineteen countries. By 2017, it had secured a seat inside the FTSE 100, the index of Britain's hundred largest publicly traded companies. At its peak in the summer of 2018, the company was worth more than eight and a half billion pounds, roughly ten to eleven billion US dollars.
Investors loved it. Analysts called it one of the Gulf's great business success stories. Its founder, B.R. Shetty, was a fixture on billionaire lists, a man whose personal narrative, from rags to the London Stock Exchange, seemed to validate the very idea that ambition could outrun circumstance.
Then, on December 14th, 2019, a US-based investment research firm called Muddy Waters Research, run by short-seller Carson Block, published a report questioning NMC's debt figures. Within four months, Britain's Financial Conduct Authority would later conclude, the company had been keeping two different sets of accounting records, and misleading the market about its true debt by as much as four billion dollars.
By April 2020, NMC Health, four million patients a year, thousands of doctors, hundreds of facilities, had collapsed into insolvency. It remains the largest corporate failure the Gulf region has ever seen. And more than six years later, the legal fight over exactly who is responsible is still being argued in court, right now, in 2026.
This is the story of how it happened.
To understand NMC Health, you have to start with the man who built it. Bavaguthu Raghuram Shetty, widely known as B.R. Shetty.
He was born in 1942 in Udupi, on India's southwestern coast, trained as a pharmacist, and moved to Abu Dhabi in the early 1970s, a time when the UAE's private healthcare sector barely existed. Most residents relied on government clinics or traveled abroad for serious treatment. Shetty saw the opening. In the mid-1970s, he opened a small clinic he called the New Medical Centre. NMC, for short.
Over the next four decades, that single clinic grew into a sprawling healthcare group. Hospitals, specialty clinics, fertility centers, and long-term care facilities across the UAE and beyond. The expansion was relentless. Shetty diversified into other industries entirely. He founded UAE Exchange, one of the region's largest money-transfer businesses, moved into pharmaceuticals, and eventually his broader business network acquired the global foreign-exchange brand Travelex.
By the 2000s, Shetty was one of the most recognizable business figures in the UAE. A man who appeared at award ceremonies, ribbon-cuttings, and formal portraits. The kind of founder whose face became synonymous with the company he built.
In April 2012, NMC Health listed its shares on the London Stock Exchange, the first UAE-based healthcare company to do so. The listing was a statement. This was no longer a regional clinic chain. It was an international enterprise, now accountable to London's rigorous disclosure standards. Five years later, in 2017, NMC was promoted into the FTSE 100. By August 2018, at its peak, the company's market value reached roughly eight point six billion pounds.
For a business that started as one clinic with a pharmacist who arrived with almost nothing, this was about as far as it could climb. The question, which no one was asking yet, was how much of that climb was real.
On December 14th, 2019, Muddy Waters Research released a report on NMC Health. The report said it believed NMC had been keeping significant debt off its official balance sheet, had potentially overpaid for some acquisitions, and had insufficiently disclosed transactions with parties connected to its own shareholders and management. Muddy Waters disclosed that it held a short position, meaning it stood to profit financially if NMC's share price fell.
NMC's response was to deny the allegations had merit, while also announcing it would launch its own independent review. The company said it was confident the review would vindicate its accounting.
The market didn't wait for the outcome. Within days, NMC's share price began falling, and it kept falling for months. Investors who had believed in the growth story were suddenly confronting the possibility that the numbers they had been reading for years might not be accurate.
In February 2020, the company's own independent review reported back, and it had found what it called potential inconsistencies in bank statements and ledger entries. That same month, NMC's Executive Vice Chairman resigned. Shortly after, B.R. Shetty himself stepped down as co-chairman, after the company disclosed that a legal review was underway to verify the actual size of his shareholding. In other words, uncertainty over exactly how much of the company he still controlled.
Within weeks, most of NMC's board had resigned or been removed. Two private equity firms briefly explored buying the company outright. Both withdrew within the month. The hospital empire that had seemed so solid was coming apart faster than anyone had predicted.
In November 2023, the UK's Financial Conduct Authority issued an official, public censure of NMC Health. This was the regulator's own final, published finding, after a multi-year investigation. The FCA concluded that between March 2019 and February 2020, NMC published financial statements containing materially inaccurate information about its debt. Specifically, the regulator found that NMC had been operating two separate, parallel sets of accounting records. And that its public financial statements had misled investors by understating the company's debt by as much as four billion dollars.
That censure was issued against the company itself for market abuse. No financial penalty was issued, because by that point the company had no remaining funds beyond what was owed to creditors.
According to the regulator's findings, part of how this happened involved a supply-chain financing arrangement, sometimes called reverse factoring. In which a subsidiary paid certain suppliers through financing facilities that were never disclosed in the company's public accounts. This mechanism allowed NMC to move debt off its balance sheet and present a healthier financial picture than the reality warranted.
By the end of March 2020, NMC's own board disclosed that the company's actual total debt was approximately six point six billion dollars. More than four billion higher than what had been reported to investors.
On April 9th, 2020, following an application by NMC's largest creditor, Abu Dhabi Commercial Bank, which was owed roughly nine hundred sixty-three million dollars, a UK High Court judge placed NMC Health into administration. NMC's own management did not oppose the move. At the hearing, the judge remarked simply that something had clearly gone very wrong with the company's management and oversight.
NMC was removed from the FTSE 100. The restructuring firm Alvarez and Marsal was appointed to run the company and try to protect what value remained for creditors.
NMC wasn't a shell company. At the time of the collapse, it operated more than two hundred facilities with over two thousand doctors, treating real patients. And it was struggling to meet payroll for its staff, in the middle of the early Covid-19 pandemic. Patients continued to walk through the doors every day, unaware that the company employing their doctors was insolvent.
A few days after the administration order, Abu Dhabi Commercial Bank filed a criminal complaint with the UAE's Attorney General's Office. Alleging fraud and forgery of financial records against several individuals connected to the company, including Shetty and NMC's former chief executive, Prasanth Manghat. The complaint alleged that forged financial statements had been used to misstate the group's liabilities.
The disruption extended beyond NMC itself. Finablr, another public company linked to Shetty and home to the UAE Exchange and Travelex brands, ran into its own severe financial difficulties and entered its own insolvency proceedings.
Shetty has said publicly and consistently that he did not know about the undisclosed debts, and that he was deceived by executives within his own companies. He has filed his own complaints with investigative authorities alleging that he too was a victim of fraud.
NMC's hospitals didn't close. In 2022, the company's core hospital and clinic operations exited administration under new ownership and continue to operate today, treating patients across the UAE. The buildings still stand. The doctors still work. The patients still arrive.
But the legal fight over responsibility for the collapse is still active right now.
In July 2023, NMC's administrators filed civil fraud lawsuits against Shetty, Manghat, and the Indian lender Bank of Baroda, seeking roughly four billion dollars. In the Abu Dhabi Global Market courts and England's High Court. Bank of Baroda has consistently denied wrongdoing.
Administrators also filed a multi-billion-pound negligence claim against NMC's former auditor, Ernst and Young, in London's High Court. Arguing the firm failed to detect the fraud during its audits. Earlier this year, that case was resolved through a confidential settlement.
In July 2026, Bank of Baroda agreed to pay six hundred million dollars to settle the claims against it, explicitly without admitting any liability or wrongdoing. That resolved the case against the bank. But the case against Shetty and Manghat continues.
In March 2026, that trial actually began, in the Abu Dhabi Global Market Courts. Both Shetty and Manghat have denied all wrongdoing. Shetty has separately filed his own eight-billion-dollar lawsuit against former executives, banks, and NMC's former auditor, arguing that he himself was the victim of a large-scale fraud carried out by people around him. As of this recording, both cases remain open, with the defendants scheduled to give evidence in the coming months.
Separately, in late 2025, a Dubai court ruled against Shetty in an unrelated financial dispute, ordering him to personally repay a guarantee he said he had never signed. With the presiding judge finding his account of events not credible.
Six years on, NMC Health is a strange kind of unfinished story. The hospitals are still open. The company itself was censured by a regulator for keeping two sets of books. But the individuals accused of responsibility are still arguing their case in an active courtroom, and every one of them has denied wrongdoing.
There's no confession, no single dramatic reveal. Just a regulator's finding that the public numbers didn't match the real ones, a trial that's still ongoing, and a founder who went from a small clinic with almost nothing behind it, to a multi-billion-dollar seat in the FTSE 100, to fighting, right now, to convince a court he didn't know what was happening inside his own company.
