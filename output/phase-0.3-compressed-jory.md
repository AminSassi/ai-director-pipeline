## "The Man Who Outprinted the Bank of England: How a Garden Shed Nearly Broke British Currency"
### The Michael Faraday £20 Note, Operation Mermaid, and the Fifty Million Pound Forgery Ring

Picture this: a twenty-pound note lands on the green felt of a London casino table. It is inspected, counted, and stacked. By morning, it sits inside the vault of the Bank of England — verified, stamped, and cleared as genuine.

Except it isn't. It never was.

That note was born days earlier in an Essex garden shed, pulled from a secondhand printing press by a quiet grandfather with no criminal record and an obsessive mastery of paper chemistry.

For four years, that shed produced the largest counterfeiting operation in British history. Tens of millions of pounds flooded the country — notes so convincing the Bank of England's own automated sorting machines stamped them genuine.

This is the story of how two men nearly broke a three-hundred-year-old institution, and how the smallest clerical mistake brought the whole thing down.

It began with a hustler and a craftsman.

Steven Jory was an East London operator selling counterfeit perfume from car boots. Cheap fragrance was poured into generic bottles, finished with fake Chanel No. 5 labels slapped on in back rooms. When Customs and Excise shut him down, Jory learned an invaluable lesson: knockoffs carried high risk for small margins. To make real fortunes, you didn't counterfeit fifty-pound bottles. You counterfeited the money used to buy them.

Jory had the ambition and the street network. What he lacked was the technical genius to defeat the most heavily guarded banknote on Earth.

That missing piece arrived through family. By the early 1990s, Jory was dating the daughter of Kenneth Mainstone.

To neighbors in Essex, Mainstone was the picture of suburban respectability: a calm family man who ran a small print shop producing business cards and leaflets. Behind that facade lay a master technician who had spent decades studying paper chemistry, lithography, and color separation. Jory saw a skill set worth millions.

It began over Sunday dinners. Casual talk drifted toward vulnerabilities in British currency. Jory joked about whether a small press could replicate real money. The challenge fascinated Mainstone; the Bank of England was the ultimate test.

Mainstone moved a commercial offset litho press into an outbuilding behind his Essex home, fitting blackout blinds and ventilation to disperse solvents. A law-abiding craftsman became the technical brain of an illicit national mint.

Their target was the Bank of England's Series D twenty-pound note, featuring Michael Faraday. Issued in 1991, Threadneedle Street considered it one of the most defended documents on Earth, assuming forgery required a paper mill and a national mint working together.

Four formidable security barriers stood in their way.

First was the paper: custom cotton-linen rag containing zero optical brighteners, staying dark under ultraviolet light while ordinary paper glows neon violet.

Second was intaglio printing: steel plates pressed thick ink into the paper, creating raised ridges on Faraday's coat anyone could feel with a thumb.

Third was the watermark: Faraday's likeness woven into the wet paper fibers during manufacturing, revealing a soft portrait when backlit.

Fourth was the metallic security thread: a silver ribbon embedded inside the pulp, appearing as broken dashes that formed a solid line against light.

For ordinary counterfeiters, these defenses were a dead end. For Mainstone, they were engineering hurdles.

Working in secret, Mainstone sourced specialty rag paper from Europe that absorbed UV light like legal tender, sizing it to replicate the crisp snap of real money.

To reproduce the intricate portrait without scanner pixelation, he used graphic reproduction cameras, breaking the Faraday note into seventeen hand-corrected color separation plates.

The intaglio feel was replicated using high-viscosity inks that dried with a tactile ridge. For the watermark, he formulated a translucent chemical solution applied between paper layers.

The metallic thread was the real obstacle. You cannot weave a thread into pre-made paper. Mainstone printed each note in two passes, bonding a thin metallic foil strip directly onto the surface while the ink cured, then overprinting the broken dashes. Held to the light, it looked identical to the real thread. A bank, a mill, and a mint — all beaten from a garden shed.

Mainstone was obsessive. For every note that left the shed, two or three imperfect sheets went straight into his incinerator. Nothing sloppy left that shed.

While Mainstone perfected the product, Jory built the pipeline. Beginning in 1994, Jory established an underground network across South London, selling bricks of fake twenties to middlemen at twenty to thirty pence on the pound.

The notes moved through the cash economy: bookmakers at dog tracks, West End casinos, crowded pubs, and petrol stations where cashiers rarely checked UV lights.

By 1996, the shed operated at industrial capacity, expanding to the Series E fifty-pound note. At their peak, investigators estimated two out of every three counterfeit notes in UK circulation came from that one shed — around fifty million pounds in face value.

And here is the terrifying part: the Bank of England's automated sorting machines, meant to catch fakes, stamped them genuine. The notes were bundled and recirculated into bank branches and cash machines nationwide.

The situation became so severe that Threadneedle Street officials debated pulling the entire Faraday note from circulation. It would have cost tens of millions of pounds and served as an embarrassing admission that their currency had failed.

Instead, authorities launched Operation Mermaid — a covert task force uniting Scotland Yard's Organised Crime Group, paper chemists, and Bank of England investigators.

Scotland Yard quickly realized chasing cash was pointless. It moved like water, with no names attached. Detectives had to trace the supply chain. Someone in Britain was buying specific supplies: non-brightened paper, specialty inks, and hot-foil equipment.

Jory's crew used fake names, front corporate addresses, burner pagers, and cash. But in industrial procurement, bureaucracy rarely forgets.

The breakthrough came from an absurdly routine clerical error.

A Midlands equipment supplier added the buyer's account to an automated marketing mailing list. Months after the operation moved, glossy trade catalogues kept arriving at the old warehouse.

New tenants threw the junk mail straight into the trash. A Scotland Yard detective, sifting through commercial waste, pulled one out.

On the mailing label was a customer reference number.

Traced through supplier records, it led detectives to a single credit card payment made before the gang switched to cash. Around the same time, investigators intercepted a counterfeit cash bundle in South London with a promotional flyer for Mainstone's print shop tucked inside, complete with a handwritten pager number for Steven Jory.

That was the whole case.

On August 14th, 1998, police launched simultaneous dawn raids. Tactical units struck an associate's base on the Isle of Wight, recovering one and a half million pounds in uncut sheets. At the same moment, officers breached the Essex garden shed, catching Mainstone red-handed with the press still warm, surrounded by inks, plates, and color separations.

In court, the conspirators diverged. Jory and his network pleaded guilty and cooperated, earning Jory an eight-year sentence.

Mainstone fought it, claiming he believed he was printing novelty movie props. The jury didn't believe a word of it. As the technical mind behind the fifty-million-pound operation, Kenneth Mainstone was sentenced to twelve years in prison — four years more than the man who recruited him.

The Bank of England was forced to rush out a redesigned twenty-pound note featuring Edward Elgar with holographic foils, costing the British economy over one hundred million pounds.

Two men in a garden shed had made a three-hundred-year-old central bank rewrite its own money. And the craftsman who mastered every kind of paper was caught by the one piece he never touched — a piece of junk mail nobody was supposed to read.
