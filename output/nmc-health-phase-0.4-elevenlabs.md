In the mid-1970s, a young pharmacist named Bavaguthu Raghuram Shetty arrived in Abu Dhabi from a small town in southern India, reportedly with only a few dollars to his name. No connections. No capital. Just a pharmacy diploma and a plan to open a single medical clinic.

Four decades later, that clinic had become NMC Health, treating four million patients a year across nineteen countries. By 2017, it was inside the FTSE 100. At its peak in 2018, the company was worth more than eight and a half billion pounds.

Investors loved it. Analysts called it one of the Gulf's great success stories. Its founder, B.R. Shetty, was a fixture on billionaire lists, a man whose personal narrative, from rags to the London Stock Exchange, seemed to validate the idea that ambition could outrun circumstance.

Then, on December 14th, 2019, a US research firm called Muddy Waters Research, run by short-seller Carson Block, published a report questioning NMC's debt figures. Within four months, Britain's Financial Conduct Authority would conclude the company had been keeping two different sets of accounting records, and misleading the market about its true debt by as much as four billion dollars.

By April 2020, NMC Health had collapsed into insolvency. It remains the largest corporate failure the Gulf region has ever seen. And more than six years later, the legal fight is still being argued in court, right now, in 2026.

This is the story of how it happened.

To understand NMC Health, you have to start with the man who built it. B.R. Shetty.

He was born in 1942 in Udupi, on India's southwestern coast, and moved to Abu Dhabi in the early 1970s, when the UAE's private healthcare sector barely existed. He opened a small clinic he called the New Medical Centre. NMC, for short.

Over the next four decades, that clinic grew into a sprawling healthcare group across the UAE and beyond. Shetty diversified into other industries. He founded UAE Exchange, one of the region's largest money-transfer businesses, and eventually his broader business network acquired the global foreign-exchange brand Travelex.

In April 2012, NMC Health listed on the London Stock Exchange, the first UAE-based healthcare company to do so. Five years later, NMC was promoted into the FTSE 100. By August 2018, at its peak, the company's market value reached roughly eight point six billion pounds.

On December 14th, 2019, Muddy Waters Research released a report on NMC Health. The report said it believed NMC had been keeping significant debt off its official balance sheet, had potentially overpaid for some acquisitions, and had insufficiently disclosed transactions with parties connected to its own shareholders and management. Muddy Waters disclosed that it held a short position, meaning it stood to profit financially if NMC's share price fell.

NMC's response was to deny the allegations had merit. The market didn't wait. Within days, share price began falling, and it kept falling for months.

In February 2020, the company's own independent review reported back, and it had found what it called potential inconsistencies in bank statements and ledger entries. That same month, NMC's Executive Vice Chairman resigned. Shortly after, B.R. Shetty himself stepped down as co-chairman, after the company disclosed that a legal review was underway to verify the actual size of his shareholding.

Within weeks, most of NMC's board had resigned or been removed. Two private equity firms briefly explored buying the company outright. Both withdrew within the month.

Once regulators actually opened the books, the numbers got a lot more specific. Stick around, because what the regulator found changes everything.

In November 2023, the UK's Financial Conduct Authority issued an official, public censure of NMC Health. The FCA concluded that between March 2019 and February 2020, NMC published financial statements containing materially inaccurate information about its debt. The regulator found that NMC had been operating two separate, parallel sets of accounting records, misleading investors by understating the company's debt by as much as four billion dollars.

That censure was issued against the company itself. No financial penalty was issued, because by that point the company had no remaining funds.

According to the regulator's findings, part of how this happened involved a supply-chain financing arrangement, sometimes called reverse factoring. In which a subsidiary paid certain suppliers through financing facilities that were never disclosed in the company's public accounts. This mechanism allowed NMC to move debt off its balance sheet and present a healthier financial picture than the reality warranted.

By the end of March 2020, NMC's own board disclosed that the company's actual total debt was approximately six point six billion dollars. More than four billion higher than what had been reported to investors.

On April 9th, 2020, following an application by NMC's largest creditor, Abu Dhabi Commercial Bank, which was owed roughly nine hundred sixty-three million dollars, a UK High Court judge placed NMC Health into administration. NMC's own management did not oppose the move. At the hearing, the judge remarked simply that something had clearly gone very wrong with the company's management and oversight.

NMC was removed from the FTSE 100. The restructuring firm Alvarez and Marsal was appointed to run the company.

NMC wasn't a shell company. At the time of the collapse, it operated more than two hundred facilities with over two thousand doctors, treating real patients. And it was struggling to meet payroll, in the middle of the early Covid-19 pandemic. Patients continued to walk through the doors every day, unaware that the company employing their doctors was insolvent.

A few days after the administration order, Abu Dhabi Commercial Bank filed a criminal complaint with the UAE's Attorney General's Office. Alleging fraud and forgery of financial records against several individuals connected to the company, including Shetty and NMC's former chief executive, Prasanth Manghat.

Shetty has said he did not know about the undisclosed debts, and that he was deceived by executives. He has filed his own complaints alleging he too was a victim of fraud.

NMC's hospitals didn't close. In 2022, the company's core operations exited administration under new ownership and continue to operate today.

But the legal fight over responsibility for the collapse is still active right now.

In July 2023, NMC's administrators filed civil fraud lawsuits against Shetty, Manghat, and the Indian lender Bank of Baroda, seeking roughly four billion dollars. Bank of Baroda has consistently denied wrongdoing.

Administrators also filed a negligence claim against NMC's former auditor, Ernst and Young, in London's High Court. Earlier this year, that case was resolved through a confidential settlement.

In July 2026, Bank of Baroda agreed to pay six hundred million dollars to settle the claims against it, explicitly without admitting any liability or wrongdoing. That resolved the case against the bank. But the case against Shetty and Manghat continues.

In March 2026, that trial actually began, in the Abu Dhabi Global Market Courts. Both Shetty and Manghat have denied all wrongdoing. Shetty has separately filed his own eight-billion-dollar lawsuit against former executives, banks, and NMC's former auditor, arguing that he himself was the victim of a large-scale fraud. As of this recording, both cases remain open.

Six years on, NMC Health is a strange kind of unfinished story. The hospitals are still open. The company itself was censured by a regulator for keeping two sets of books. But the individuals accused of responsibility are still arguing their case in an active courtroom, and every one of them has denied wrongdoing.
