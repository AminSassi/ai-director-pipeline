## [0:00 – 0:50] — HOOK

In 2013, a movie about greed, fraud, and Wall Street excess hit theaters worldwide. It starred Leonardo DiCaprio. It was nominated for five Oscars. It was called "The Wolf of Wall Street."

Here's what almost nobody knew at the time: a huge chunk of the money used to make that movie was allegedly stolen. Not stolen from a bank. Stolen from an entire country.

A young financier siphoned billions of dollars out of a government fund meant to build hospitals, schools, and infrastructure for millions of ordinary people — and used it to bankroll superyachts, a private jet, jewelry for supermodels, parties with A-list celebrities, and yes, a movie about financial fraud, paid for with the proceeds of financial fraud.

The prime minister at the center of it all has now been sentenced to prison twice. The man who orchestrated the whole scheme has been missing for years, and nobody — not the FBI, not Interpol, nobody — has been able to find him.

This is the story of 1MDB, one of the largest financial heists in world history. And it starts with two teenagers at a private school in England.

## [1:20 – 3:00] — THE SETUP

In the late 1990s, a Malaysian teenager named Jho Low was sent to Harrow, one of Britain's most elite boarding schools, to prepare for university. At Harrow, he became close friends with another student: Riza Aziz, the stepson of a rising Malaysian politician named Najib Razak.

That friendship would eventually help move billions of dollars out of a country and into some of the most expensive parties the world has ever seen.

Fast forward to 2009. Najib Razak becomes Prime Minister of Malaysia. Almost immediately, he sets up a new government investment fund. Its name: 1Malaysia Development Berhad — 1MDB, for short. On paper, its mission sounds almost boring: attract foreign investment, fund infrastructure, drive economic growth for the Malaysian people.

In reality, prosecutors would later say, it became something closer to a personal piggy bank — run with the help of Najib's old friend from Harrow, a man who, remarkably, never held any official position inside the fund at all.

That man was Jho Low.

## [3:00 – 6:00] — THE SCHEME

Here's where it gets almost too outrageous to believe.

In 2009, 1MDB entered a joint venture with a Saudi oil company. The Malaysian fund put in one billion dollars of public money. Almost immediately — practically overnight — around seven hundred million dollars of that billion was quietly rerouted into the account of a shell company with an innocent-sounding name: Good Star Limited.

That company had one function. To move stolen money somewhere it couldn't easily be traced.

Over the next few years, 1MDB kept raising more and more money — this time through massive bond sales, arranged with the help of Goldman Sachs, one of the most respected investment banks in the world. Goldman helped 1MDB raise over six billion dollars. And for arranging those bonds, Goldman collected fees that were, by some estimates, dramatically higher than what banks typically charge for this kind of work — fees so unusually large that they would later become a central piece of evidence in the case against the bank itself.

Billions of dollars were now flowing through 1MDB. And billions of dollars were quietly flowing right back out — into shell companies, private accounts, and a growing global spending spree that nobody in the Malaysian public had any idea was happening.

Meanwhile, the real Jho Low was becoming something else entirely: a legend on the international party circuit. Nightclub owners in Las Vegas and New York knew him as the guy who'd casually spend hundreds of thousands of dollars on champagne in a single night. He partied with Paris Hilton. He reportedly gifted a Picasso and a Basquiat worth millions of dollars combined. He chartered superyachts. He bought real estate in Beverly Hills, New York, and London.

And in 2012, he did something that would eventually make this entire scandal impossible to ignore: he helped finance a Martin Scorsese film.

## [6:00 – 8:10] — THE MOVIE, THE PARTIES, THE PROOF

Riza Aziz — Najib's stepson, Jho Low's old friend from Harrow — had started a Hollywood production company called Red Granite Pictures. In 2013, that company produced "The Wolf of Wall Street," starring Leonardo DiCaprio, based on the true story of a real Wall Street stockbroker who defrauded investors.

Prosecutors later alleged that a significant share of that film's budget can be traced back to money diverted from 1MDB.

To be clear: Leonardo DiCaprio was never accused of knowingly taking part in the fraud. When the allegations became public, he cooperated with investigators, and both he and his foundation returned gifts they had received — including a Marlon Brando Oscar statuette and pieces of art — that were later tied to 1MDB money. He wasn't the one who stole anything. He was, in a very real sense, a person who ended up close to the story almost by accident, because of who was funding the film he starred in.

But the irony of the situation is hard to overstate. A movie warning the world about the seductive danger of financial fraud was, allegedly, partly paid for with the actual proceeds of financial fraud.

By the early 2010s, roughly 4.5 billion dollars had allegedly been misappropriated from 1MDB. Investigators say around a billion of that ended up flowing into Najib Razak's own personal bank accounts.

And for years — years — almost nobody outside a very small circle knew any of it.

## [8:50 – 11:00] — THE UNRAVELING

In 2015, 1MDB missed a loan payment. A relatively small one, in the scheme of things — a few hundred million dollars. But it was enough to make journalists start asking questions.

That July, the Wall Street Journal published a bombshell: nearly seven hundred million dollars had allegedly flowed into Najib Razak's personal bank accounts. Around the same time, a journalist named Clare Rewcastle-Brown, running a small investigative outlet called the Sarawak Report, began publishing leaked financial documents that laid out the scheme in far greater detail than anyone had seen before.

Najib denied everything. He claimed the money in his accounts was a political donation from a Saudi royal family — not stolen public funds.

Malaysia's government moved fast to control the damage: the attorney general who was investigating the case was replaced. Journalists covering the story faced pressure. For a while, it looked like Najib might simply ride it out.

But the story had already escaped Malaysia's borders. Investigators in the United States, Switzerland, Singapore, and half a dozen other countries started pulling on the thread — because the money hadn't just moved through Malaysia. It had moved through the American and international banking system, through shell companies registered in tax havens, through art auctions and yacht purchases that left a paper trail an ocean wide.

In 2016, the U.S. Department of Justice filed civil lawsuits to seize more than a billion dollars in assets connected to the scandal — at the time, the largest single action of its kind in the Department's history. Real estate. A private jet. Artwork. Royalties from a movie that had already made hundreds of millions of dollars at the box office.

Jho Low, the man at the center of it all, saw where this was heading. And he did the one thing that made this story impossible to ever fully close.

He vanished.

---

## [11:00 – 13:00] — THE FALL

In 2018, Najib Razak called a general election, likely hoping to secure enough political power to make the entire scandal disappear. Instead, Malaysian voters did something that hadn't happened in over sixty years: they voted his party out of power entirely.

Weeks after losing the election, Najib was arrested.

In 2020, he was convicted on seven charges — including criminal breach of trust, abuse of power, and money laundering — related to a smaller piece of the 1MDB puzzle involving roughly ten million dollars. He was sentenced to twelve years in prison. He appealed. He lost.

In August 2022, he became the first former Prime Minister in Malaysian history to actually walk into a prison cell.

That same year, Goldman Sachs — the bank that had helped raise the billions that fueled this entire scheme — agreed to pay roughly 2.9 billion dollars in penalties to authorities in the U.S., Malaysia, and elsewhere. Its Malaysian subsidiary pleaded guilty to criminal charges.

For one of the most powerful investment banks on Wall Street, it was a staggering admission.

Riza Aziz, the Hollywood producer, was charged in Malaysia with laundering nearly a quarter of a billion dollars — charges that were later dropped after he agreed to return assets to the government, a decision that triggered public outrage in Malaysia.

And Jho Low? To this day, he has never been caught. He's believed to be hiding somewhere in China, protected — some investigators suspect — by connections that go far beyond a single missing financier. He has an active Interpol Red Notice. A Kuwaiti court sentenced him, in absentia, to ten years. He has never set foot in a courtroom to answer for any of it.

## [13:00 – 14:10] — WHERE THINGS STAND RIGHT NOW

Here's the part of this story that most people don't even know is still unfolding.

In 2024, a Malaysian pardons board unexpectedly cut Najib's twelve-year sentence in half, down to six years — a decision that sparked accusations of political favoritism at the highest levels.

Then, in December 2025 — just a few months ago as we're recording this — Najib was convicted again, this time in a separate trial covering more than two billion Malaysian ringgit in 1MDB-linked charges. He was sentenced to an additional fifteen years.

Since then, Najib has been fighting a legal battle to serve the rest of his sentence under house arrest instead of behind bars, claiming a secret royal order entitles him to it. As of this year, Malaysia's courts have rejected that request. He remains in Kajang Prison.

One of the biggest financial scandals in modern history — and the legal fallout from it is still actively happening, right now, while you're watching this.
