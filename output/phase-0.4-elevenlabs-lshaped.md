Meet Professor Motoyuki Sato. A radar engineer from Tohoku University in Japan. For decades, he's been developing technology that can see through solid ground—without digging a single hole.

In early 2025, Sato and his team arrived at the Giza Plateau alongside Egyptian archaeologists. They weren't there to excavate. They were there to scan.

They focused on the Western Cemetery—a vast burial ground west of the Great Pyramid of Khufu, known for its mastabas: flat-roofed tombs of ancient Egyptian nobility. But one section had never been properly explored. A flat, empty stretch of sand. No structures on the surface. Nothing to indicate anything was beneath it.

The team deployed two technologies: Ground-Penetrating Radar, or GPR, and Electrical Resistivity Tomography, or ERT.

GPR works by sending radar waves into the ground. When those waves hit something solid, they bounce back to the surface. By measuring the return time, scientists can create a 3D map of what lies beneath. ERT sends electrical currents into the ground and measures how easily they travel through different materials. Dense stone conducts differently than sand. And empty space—a void—conducts differently than everything else.

Together, these technologies can see through the earth without disturbing a single grain of sand.

And what they found shocked everyone.

---

About two meters beneath the surface—roughly 6.5 feet down—the radar detected something unusual. A structure with sharp, defined angles. An L-shape, approximately 10 meters long and 10 meters wide.

Natural formations don't create perfect right angles. Sand shifts randomly. Rocks fracture unpredictably. But this shape was precise. Deliberate.

The researchers, publishing in the journal Archaeological Prospection, wrote: "The sharp, defined angles of this formation suggest that it is man-made."

But that's not all.

Beneath the L-shape—at a depth of 5 to 10 meters, or 16 to 33 feet—the team found something even more intriguing. A second anomaly. A large, highly electrically resistive area.

What does that mean? The material down there is different from the surrounding ground. It could be a mixture of sand and gravel. Or... it could be an air void.

An air void. A hollow space. A chamber.

The researchers can't see inside it yet. The radar can't penetrate deep enough to determine exactly what it is. But they have a theory.

Professor Sato believes the L-shaped structure could be an entrance. A doorway. A passage leading down to something much larger.

And if that's true... what's waiting at the bottom?

To understand what this means, we need to understand where it was found.

The Western Cemetery is 4,500 years old. The burial ground of Egypt's royal family and high officials. The tombs here—the mastabas—are rectangular, flat-roofed structures, aligned in precise rows.

The L-shaped structure matches none of them. It's not aligned with the other tombs. It's different. Unique. Out of place.

So what could it be?

Some experts believe it could be an undiscovered tomb—perhaps the burial site of someone important whose final resting place was lost to history. Others suggest a passageway: a corridor leading to a deeper chamber untouched for millennia. And then there's the most intriguing possibility—that this L-shape is just the tip of something far larger. A complex of underground structures beneath Giza.

Dr. Roland Enmarch, an Egyptologist at the University of Liverpool, told the BBC: "It would not surprise me if it turned out to be a tomb of some sort."

But on the deeper anomaly, he was more cautious: "At present, there just isn't enough information to know whether it is a natural feature or man-made."

The mystery remains unsolved. For now.

But not everyone is convinced.

In March 2025, a separate group of Italian researchers made far more extraordinary claims. Using satellite-based radar, they said they'd detected massive underground structures beneath the Pyramid of Khafre—cylindrical shafts, spiral passages, and chambers stretching thousands of feet underground.

These claims went viral. Social media exploded with theories about ancient energy grids and the legendary Hall of Records. But experts pushed back. National Geographic questioned the Italian team's methodology. Scientists noted that satellite radar has serious limitations—it cannot see as deep or as clearly as ground-penetrating radar.

Here's the crucial difference: the Japanese team's discovery is based on ground-level scans. Published in a peer-reviewed journal. Modest in scope—a 10-meter structure, not a massive underground city.

Professor Sato's team isn't making wild claims. They're saying: "We found something strange. We don't know what it is. But it's man-made, and it's worth investigating."
