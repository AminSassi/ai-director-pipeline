2001. Adam Neumann arrives in New York. Twenty-two, six-foot-five, fresh out of five years in the Israeli Navy. No money, no connections, no plan. Just an American dream the size of the sky.

His first ventures failed, from collapsible heels to baby clothes with rubber knee pads. But in 2008, after the crash, Neumann and architect Miguel McKelvey noticed empty office floors everywhere, while freelancers were desperate for desks.

In 2010, they founded WeWork in SoHo: sign long-term commercial leases at wholesale, renovate, and sublease desks on flexible month-to-month contracts.

Firms like Regus had done it for decades. But Neumann sold it as a revolution.

He wasn't selling offices. He was selling 'community' and 'elevating the world's consciousness.'

Investors bought it completely. Neumann pitched barefoot, talking about spirituality and meditation, making venture capitalists feel part of something bigger than money.

By 2014, WeWork operated in twenty-three cities. By 2016, one hundred. Growth was insane. But beneath neon signs and free beer lay a fatal problem.

The company was incinerating cash. Between March 2018 and March 2019, WeWork lost two hundred and nineteen thousand dollars an hour. Every hour. Every day.

Yet Neumann wasn't worried. He found someone with bottomless pockets: Masayoshi Son, the billionaire running SoftBank.

In 2017, SoftBank made its first massive bet: four point four billion dollars. Masayoshi Son, who turned twenty million into fifty billion on Alibaba, had raised the hundred-billion-dollar Vision Fund and believed WeWork was the next global monopoly.

Their first meeting lasted under thirty minutes. Son told Neumann: 'In a fight between crazy people, the crazier one wins. You are crazy, but you make me look sane. You need to think much bigger.'

In January 2019, SoftBank pumped another two billion dollars in, rocketing WeWork's valuation to forty-seven billion dollars.

This valuation was an optical illusion. It wasn't based on profits or cash flows, but on SoftBank's investment alone.

Like a wealthy friend buying a soccer ball for one thousand dollars, then telling the world it's worth a thousand dollars. Everyone believes it because the friend is rich. But the ball is still just a ball.

WeWork was an office subleasing company. But Son's money made it look like a diamond.

Armed with billions, Neumann began living a life that defied belief.

Now, let's take a look at what Adam Neumann actually did with the money.

First: the private jet. Neumann bought a Gulfstream G650ER for sixty million dollars with company funds, turning it into a flying party.

On one transatlantic flight, marijuana smoke forced crew members into cockpit oxygen masks. On another to Mexico City, passengers spat tequila and vomited across the cabin, leaving zero tip.

Second: the drink rider. Twelve cases of Don Julio 1942 tequila, forty-eight bottles of kosher wine, two hundred and sixteen beers, and two bottles of thirty-year Highland Park scotch at one thousand dollars each. The bill exceeded an entry-level worker's annual salary.

Third: 'Summer Camp.' Originally on an upstate New York farm with yoga, axe-throwing, drum circles, and drugs, flying in The Weeknd by helicopter.

In 2018, they moved to London, flying eight thousand employees worldwide. Staff received two bottles of rosé upon arrival, with lakeside meditation, Lorde, and speeches from Deepak Chopra. Former employees described an unhinged frat party, with people drunk on the grass and sounds of sex from neighboring tents.

Fourth: the luxury office. A boxing bag, gong, bar, sauna, cold plunge, and ceiling 'smoke eater' for marijuana.

Neumann rode an electric skateboard down hallways and fired employees giving off 'negative energy.' This wasn't a company. It was a corporate cult run by a man who believed he was destined to live forever, eliminate world hunger, and become the world's first trillionaire.

In August 2019, WeWork filed its Form S-1 with the SEC to prepare for an IPO. It was marketed as Neumann's coronation.

Instead, when Wall Street opened the books, disaster struck.

The filing opened dedicating itself to 'the energy of we,' but revealed a nightmare. In 2018, WeWork lost one point nine billion dollars on revenues of one point eight billion. For every dollar brought in, it burned two.

Worse, the filing exposed a fatal flaw: WeWork signed forty-seven billion dollars in long-term lease liabilities, while revenue came from casual members who could cancel on thirty days' notice. If the economy stumbled, WeWork would be crushed.

The filing also revealed shocking self-dealing. Neumann owned buildings privately, leased them to WeWork, and collected millions in rent. He even trademarked 'We' and sold it back to WeWork for nearly six million dollars in stock.

Investors panicked. In six weeks, WeWork's valuation crashed from forty-seven billion dollars to ten billion. The IPO was canceled.

In mid-September, the board ousted Neumann as CEO. But he didn't leave empty-handed. SoftBank handed him a one-point-seven billion dollar exit package — including two hundred and forty-five million in cash and one hundred and eighty-five million in consulting fees.

The man who destroyed forty billion dollars in valuation and once burned office furniture at a party was handed a fortune for failure.

In October 2019, SoftBank bailed out WeWork with an emergency nine-point-five billion dollar package. Masayoshi Son later admitted his faith in Neumann was a humiliating error.

WeWork tried to survive under new management. In 2021, it merged with a SPAC at a nine-billion-dollar valuation. But the structural wounds were fatal.

When remote work surged, WeWork's lease commitments became an inescapable trap. By 2023, the stock was down ninety-eight percent, and the company was worth under forty-five million dollars — while debt exceeded eighteen billion dollars.

On November 6th, 2023, WeWork filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

The filing listed fifteen billion dollars in assets against eighteen point six billion in liabilities. Thousands of employees lost their jobs, seeing stock options wiped out. Hundreds of landlords were left holding billions in unpaid rent. The dream was dead.

So what happened to Adam Neumann?

He was never charged with a crime. As of 2026, his net worth is estimated at two point eight billion dollars. He lives in luxury across Manhattan and the Hamptons. And in proof venture capital never learns, Neumann launched Flow — raising three hundred and fifty million dollars from Andreessen Horowitz before launching.

The WeWork saga is the definitive story of mass delusion in modern capitalism. How charisma, alcohol, and empty promises convinced the smartest investors to hand over billions.

Masayoshi Son poured sixteen billion dollars into WeWork and lost most of it, alongside his reputation. Neumann walked away with a fortune. Because in modern startup finance, it doesn't matter how much you lose. What matters is how much you take with you when you fall.
