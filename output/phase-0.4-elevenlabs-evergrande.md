Hui Ka Yan was born in 1958 in Henan province, raised by his grandmother in poverty after his mother died in infancy.

He worked fields, failed China's college entrance exam once, then studied metallurgy at Wuhan Institute of Iron and Steel before spending a decade in a state-owned steel mill.

In 1992, he quit his government job, packed three thousand dollars in savings, and headed for Shenzhen to chase China's real estate boom under Deng Xiaoping's mantra: to get rich is glorious. In 1996, in Guangzhou, he founded Evergrande.

The model was simple: sell apartments before building them. Collect buyer cash upfront, fund construction, and buy the next plot of land. In China, ninety percent of new homes were sold this way. It worked as long as growth continued.

Evergrande went public in Hong Kong in 2009. Within a decade, it was the world's biggest property developer by sales, sitting on land holdings five times the size of Manhattan.

Hui expanded into football, bottled water, insurance, and electric vehicles, vowing to surpass Tesla. By 2017, his fortune peaked at forty-two billion dollars, making him Asia's richest man. He bought global mansions and ran with Hong Kong tycoons in the "poker club."

On paper, Hui was proof Deng's promise had come true. In reality, the empire stood on borrowed money — and the bill was coming due.

In August 2020, Chinese regulators introduced the "Three Red Lines" policy, capping debt relative to assets, equity, and short-term cash. Evergrande failed all three.

Cheap credit dried up, and the structure buckled. By August 2021, construction halted nationwide as suppliers went unpaid. China's central bank took the unusual step of publicly warning Evergrande. In September, the company missed two overseas bond payments totaling 131 million dollars. Panicked investors and homebuyers protested outside Evergrande's Shenzhen headquarters until security hauled them away.

While ordinary families watched their life savings vanish, Hui Ka Yan quietly sold off 1.2 billion of his own Evergrande shares that November, cashing out roughly 340 million dollars and cutting his stake from seventy-seven percent to sixty-eight. The captain was untying his own lifeboat first.

By December 2021, Evergrande formally defaulted on its offshore debt — one of the largest corporate defaults in Asian financial history.

Here's what a corporate default looks like when you zoom in on real people.

Because ninety percent of Chinese homes are pre-sold, an estimated 1.6 million families had already paid life savings for Evergrande apartments that simply stopped being built. Buyers across a hundred cities launched mass mortgage boycotts, refusing to pay loans on homes they could never move into. Nomura later estimated twenty million housing units sat unfinished nationwide, with a funding gap over four hundred billion dollars.

These abandoned complexes earned a grim nickname: "lanwei lou" — rotten-tail buildings. Rows of concrete skeletons sat fenced off with cheerful banners rotting in the wind. At Central Plaza in Shijiazhuang, eighteen hundred buyers who paid over three hundred fifty thousand dollars each were left waiting with no finished home in sight.

By the time the dust settled, Evergrande's total liabilities were confirmed at more than three hundred billion dollars — more than the entire GDP of Portugal or New Zealand.

By August 2025, the company was delisted from the Hong Kong exchange, its stock crashing to sixteen cents from a peak valuation of fifty billion dollars.

In April 2026, after years of investigation, Hui pleaded guilty to financial fraud, bribery, illegally taking public deposits, and fraudulently issuing securities.
