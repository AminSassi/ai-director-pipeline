[ACT 1 — THE RISE]
Hui Ka Yan was born in 1958, in a small farming village in China's Henan province. His mother died when he was eight months old. He was raised almost entirely by his grandmother, in what his own biographers describe as genuine poverty.

He worked the fields as a teenager. He worked as a security guard. He failed China's brutal national college entrance exam once, passed it on his second attempt, and studied metallurgy at the Wuhan Institute of Iron and Steel — surviving on scholarships and stipends. After graduating, he spent nearly a decade working inside a state-owned steel mill.

Then, in 1992, he did something that would have been unthinkable for his grandmother's generation. He quit his stable government job, packed less than three thousand dollars in savings, and headed for Shenzhen — the boomtown right across the border from Hong Kong — to chase China's newly-opened real estate market. It was the era of Deng Xiaoping's famous line: "to get rich is glorious." Hui took it literally.

In 1996, in the city of Guangzhou, he founded a company called Evergrande.

The business model was deceptively simple, and it's the same model almost every developer in China used: sell the apartment before you build it. Collect the buyer's money up front. Use that cash to fund construction — and to buy the next plot of land for the next tower. In China, this wasn't some fringe scheme. Roughly ninety percent of new homes were sold this way. It worked beautifully — as long as the growth never stopped.

And for two decades, it didn't. Evergrande went public on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange in 2009, becoming the largest privately-owned mainland Chinese developer ever listed there. Within another decade, it was the biggest property developer on Earth by sales, sitting on land holdings roughly five times the size of Manhattan.

Hui didn't stop at apartments. He built Guangzhou Evergrande into one of the most dominant football clubs in Chinese history. He launched a bottled water brand. He moved into insurance, banking, entertainment — and, in one of the boldest bets of his career, electric vehicles, publicly vowing to build "the most powerful new energy automobile company in the world" and to overtake Tesla.

By 2017, his personal fortune peaked at somewhere between forty-two and forty-five billion dollars. He was, by some rankings, the richest man in Asia. He bought mansions around the world — including one in Sydney so extravagant that Australian regulators later forced him to sell it for violating the country's foreign-investment rules. He ran in the same circles as Hong Kong's most powerful tycoons, in a tight-knit group nicknamed the "poker club."

On paper, Hui Ka Yan was the ultimate proof that Deng Xiaoping's promise had come true.

In reality, the entire empire was standing on a foundation of borrowed money — and the bill was about to come due.

[ACT 2 — THE CRACKS]
In August 2020, Chinese regulators sat down with the country's biggest property developers — Evergrande among them — and introduced a new policy nicknamed the "Three Red Lines." It measured just three things: how much debt a company carried relative to its assets, relative to its own equity, and relative to the cash it actually had on hand to cover short-term bills.

Evergrande failed all three.

That single fact told regulators — and, eventually, the entire world — that this company had been borrowing at a scale that no longer made sense. And once the cheap credit dried up, the entire structure that Hui had built for two decades started to buckle almost immediately.

By August 2021, construction was grinding to a halt on projects across the country because contractors and suppliers simply weren't being paid. China's central bank took the unusual step of publicly warning Evergrande to get its debt under control. By September, the company missed two overseas bond payments totaling 131 million dollars. Panic set in. Investors and homebuyers, terrified they'd lose everything, showed up outside Evergrande's headquarters in Shenzhen, chanting for their money back — until security personnel physically hauled them away from the building.

And here's the detail that should make your blood boil. While all of this was happening — while ordinary families were realizing their life savings might vanish — Hui Ka Yan quietly sold off 1.2 billion of his own Evergrande shares that same November, cashing out roughly 340 million dollars for himself and cutting his ownership stake from seventy-seven percent down to sixty-eight.

The captain wasn't going down with the ship. He was untying his own lifeboat first.

By December 2021, it was official: Evergrande formally defaulted on its offshore debt. At the time, it was one of the largest corporate defaults in the history of Asian financial markets.

[ACT 3 — THE HUMAN COST]
Here's what a corporate default actually looks like when you zoom in on real people.

Remember — roughly ninety percent of new homes in China are sold before they're even built. So when a company the size of Evergrande collapses, it isn't just bondholders and banks who get hurt. By the middle of 2022, an estimated one point six million Chinese families had already paid — in some cases, their entire life savings — for Evergrande apartments that simply stopped being built.

Buyers in more than a hundred cities coordinated something almost unheard of in China: mass "mortgage boycotts," refusing to keep paying loans on homes they could never move into. Independent analysts at the investment bank Nomura later estimated that, across the entire country, roughly twenty million housing units sat unfinished because of Evergrande and other failed developers — with an estimated funding gap of over four hundred billion dollars just to finish what was already started.

These half-built, abandoned complexes earned their own grim nickname in Chinese: "lanwei lou" — rotten-tail buildings. They became the visual symbol of the entire crisis: rows of concrete skeletons, fenced off, with cheerful banners still hanging on the gates reading things like "Happy Every Day," slowly rotting in the wind.

Take one project in Shijiazhuang — the Central Plaza development. It promised eighteen hundred new homes. Construction stopped in 2021. Years later, buyers who paid over three hundred fifty thousand dollars for units there were still watching, and waiting, with no finished apartment in sight.

By the time the dust settled, Evergrande's total liabilities were confirmed at more than three hundred billion dollars. That's not a typo. Three hundred billion dollars — more than the entire GDP of countries like Portugal or New Zealand — owed by a single real estate company.

[ACT 4 — RUNNING OUT THE CLOCK]
As the walls closed in, the story stopped being just about spreadsheets — and started looking a lot more like a heist film.

In January 2023, Evergrande's own auditor — the global accounting giant PwC — publicly resigned, citing unresolved disagreements over the company's 2021 financial statements. Translation: the professionals whose entire job was to check whether these books were honest looked at them and walked away rather than put their name on them.

Then came the divorce. In 2022, Hui Ka Yan and his wife of decades, Ding Yumei, quietly finalized a split that Hong Kong lawyers would later describe as a "technical divorce" — one that just happened to move a significant chunk of family assets out of Hui's name, right as investigators started circling.

In July 2023, Ding boarded a flight from Hong Kong straight to Canada. One month later, she had Canadian citizenship — a country with no extradition treaty with mainland China. Liquidators are still fighting her in a UK courtroom right now, trying to claw back nearly three hundred sixty million dollars in dividends she received in the years before the collapse.

And Hui himself? In September 2023, Chinese police finally took him into custody, placing him under so-called "residential surveillance" — his first real disappearance from public life in over three decades. A luxury mansion he owned on Hong Kong's most exclusive hillside had already been seized by his own bank the year before.

Even the electric-car dream — the one meant to make him "bigger than Tesla" — collapsed in almost comical fashion. After pouring in untold billions of dollars, Evergrande's EV division had, by the middle of 2024, delivered a grand total of one thousand, four hundred and twenty-nine vehicles.

In January 2024, after two years of failed restructuring talks, a Hong Kong court formally ordered Evergrande into liquidation — one of the largest corporate liquidations the world has ever seen. By August 2025, the company was delisted from the Hong Kong Stock Exchange entirely, quietly ending a sixteen-year run that once made it the largest privately-owned mainland developer ever listed there. Its final share price: sixteen cents. Its peak market value, back in 2017, had been roughly fifty billion dollars.

[ACT 5 — THE RECKONING]
In April 2026, after years of investigation, Hui Ka Yan pleaded guilty in a Chinese court to a genuinely staggering list of charges: financial fraud, bribery, illegally taking public deposits, misusing company funds, and fraudulently issuing securities.

At the center of it all was one number. Regulators found that Evergrande's main mainland subsidiary had inflated its reported revenue by close to eighty billion dollars across just two years — 2019 and 2020 alone. To put that in perspective: that's more than a hundred times the size of the accounting fraud that brought down Enron.

Then, on August 20th, 2026 — just weeks before this video — a court in Shenzhen delivered its final verdict. Hui Ka Yan: life in prison. Every personal asset he had left: ordered confiscated. His two sons, along with more than fifty other Evergrande executives, were sentenced right alongside him, with terms ranging from twenty-two months to eighteen years. Evergrande's companies were hit with combined fines of over two billion dollars.

According to reporters who covered the trial, there weren't many tears shed that day — least of all among the millions of ordinary families who had handed this company their life savings for apartments that, for many of them, still don't exist.

[THE TAKEAWAY]
Here's the uncomfortable truth this story leaves you with: a life sentence doesn't rebuild a single apartment.

Liquidators are still hunting for Hui's remaining money across Hong Kong, the UK, and offshore accounts, trying to recover roughly six billion dollars in dividends and salary that he and his inner circle pulled out of the company in its final "healthy" years. His ex-wife is still fighting a frozen-assets battle in a UK courtroom. PwC — the very auditor that once signed off on these books before quietly resigning — is still in Hong Kong court, defending its own role in the disaster. And out there right now, in cities across China, there are still families standing outside fenced-off construction sites, looking at the empty shell of the home they already paid for in full.

The company Hui Ka Yan built with less than three thousand dollars in savings once controlled enough land to cover the island of Manhattan five times over.

Today, it's gone. What's left behind is one of the largest unpaid bills, and one of the largest broken promises, in the history of the global economy.
