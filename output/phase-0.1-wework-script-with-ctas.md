## [1:30 – 4:30] — ACT ONE: THE RISE — BAREFOOT TO THE TOP

Let's go back to the beginning. 2001. Adam Neumann arrives in New York. He's twenty-two years old, energetic, six-foot-five, fresh out of five years in the Israeli Navy. He has no money, no connections, and no plan. But he carries one thing: an American dream the size of the sky.

His first business ideas in New York were chaotic failures, ranging from collapsible women's high heels to baby clothes fitted with rubber knee pads. But in 2008, following the financial crash, Neumann noticed something alongside an architect named Miguel McKelvey: empty office floors were everywhere, while independent freelancers were desperate for desks.

In 2010, Neumann co-founds WeWork with Miguel McKelvey in SoHo. The core idea is simple: sign long-term commercial leases with landlords at wholesale prices, renovate the spaces, and sublease individual desks to entrepreneurs on flexible month-to-month contracts.

Nothing technologically new here. Regus had been running that exact same business for decades. But the way Neumann sold it was revolutionary.

He wasn't selling offices. He was selling 'elevating the world's consciousness.' He was selling 'community.' He was selling a complete transformation in how humans coexist and work.

And investors bought it completely. Not because the balance sheet made sense, but because Neumann was brilliant at selling the vision. He would show up to high-stakes boardroom meetings barefoot, talk about spirituality, kabbalah, and meditation, and make hard-nosed venture capitalists feel like they were participating in something far bigger than money.

By 2014, WeWork was operating across twenty-three cities worldwide. By 2016, that footprint reached one hundred cities. The growth was insane. But beneath the neon signs and free craft beer, there was one fatal problem.

The company was losing money. A catastrophic amount of money.

Between March 2018 and March 2019, WeWork was losing two hundred and nineteen thousand dollars an hour. Every single hour. Twenty-four hours a day, every single day.

Yet Neumann wasn't worried in the slightest. Because he found someone with bottomless pockets to fund his dream: Masayoshi Son, the legendary Japanese billionaire who runs SoftBank.

## [4:30 – 7:00] — ACT TWO: THE DEVIL'S DEAL — SOFTBANK AND SON

In 2017, SoftBank made its first massive bet on WeWork: four point four billion dollars. Masayoshi Son, who had made historic returns from an early twenty-million-dollar investment in Alibaba, had just raised the hundred-billion-dollar Vision Fund and was convinced WeWork was the next global tech monopoly.

Their first meeting in New York lasted less than thirty minutes. Son told Neumann: 'In a fight between crazy people, the crazier one wins. You are crazy, but you make me look sane. You need to think much bigger.' And he meant it as a compliment.

In January 2019, SoftBank pumped another two billion dollars in, and with that single private check, WeWork's valuation skyrocketed to forty-seven billion dollars.

Let's make something completely clear: this valuation was an optical illusion. It wasn't based on operating profits, margins, or sustainable cash flows. It was based entirely on SoftBank's private investment alone.

It's like your wealthy friend buys an ordinary soccer ball from you for one thousand dollars, then tells the world: 'Look, my soccer ball is worth a thousand dollars!' Everyone believes it because the friend is rich. But the ball is still just an ordinary ball.

WeWork was an office subleasing company. But Son's money made it look like a diamond.

And armed with billions in cash, Neumann started living a life that defied belief.

## [7:00 – 10:30] — ACT THREE: THE EMPEROR'S LIFE — TEQUILA, PLANES, AND TIGERS

Let's take a tour of Adam Neumann's life. Because what he did with investor money deserves a Hollywood movie.

First: the private jet. Neumann bought a Gulfstream G650ER jet for sixty million dollars — paid for directly with company funds. He didn't just use it for business travel; he turned it into a flying party.

On one flight across the Atlantic, there was so much marijuana smoke in the cabin that the flight crew had to put on emergency oxygen masks. On another charter flight to Mexico City, passengers were spitting tequila at each other, leaving vomit all over the luxury cabin. And when the jet landed, they didn't even leave a tip for the crew.

Second: the drink list. Neumann had a three-and-a-half-page personal hospitality rider for his travels. It required twelve cases of vintage Don Julio 1942 tequila, forty-eight bottles of red and white kosher wine, two hundred and sixteen bottles of beer, and two bottles of thirty-year-old Highland Park scotch at one thousand dollars each. That drink list alone was enough to cover the full annual salary of an entry-level WeWork employee.

Third: the 'Summer Camp' parties. Every year, WeWork threw a massive company festival. In the beginning, it was held at a farm in upstate New York, featuring yoga, axe-throwing, drum circles, and drugs. One year, they flew in pop star The Weeknd by private helicopter for a private concert.

In 2018, they moved the spectacle to London, flying in eight thousand employees from all over the world. Staff were handed two bottles of rosé upon arrival, enjoying performances by Lorde, lakeside meditation sessions, beatboxing workshops, and speeches from Deepak Chopra. Former employees described it as an unhinged frat party, with people drunk on the grass and the sounds of sex coming from neighboring tents.

Fourth: the luxury office. In Neumann's Manhattan office suite, there was a boxing bag, a ceremonial gong, a private bar, a sauna, and a cold plunge. Because he smoked weed during the workday, the company installed an industrial 'smoke eater' in the ceiling to suck marijuana smoke out of his office.

Neumann rode an electric skateboard down corporate hallways and fired employees on the spot if he felt they gave off 'negative energy.' This wasn't a company. This was a corporate cult run by a man who believed he was destined to live forever, eliminate world hunger, and become the world's first trillionaire.

## [10:30 – 13:00] — ACT FOUR: THE COLLAPSE — SIX WEEKS THAT SHOOK WALL STREET

In August 2019, WeWork officially filed its Form S-1 with the SEC to prepare for an IPO. It was marketed as the crowning achievement of Neumann's career.

Instead, when Wall Street analysts opened the books, disaster struck.

The prospectus opened with a bizarre dedication to 'the energy of we,' but the financial statements revealed a nightmare. In 2018 alone, WeWork lost one point nine billion dollars on revenues of one point eight billion. For every dollar the company took in, it was incinerating more than two.

Worse, the filing exposed a fatal structural flaw: WeWork had signed forty-seven billion dollars in non-cancelable long-term lease liabilities with landlords, while its revenue came from casual members who could cancel their desks on thirty days' notice. If the economy stumbled, WeWork would be crushed.

The filing also revealed shocking self-dealing. Neumann owned commercial buildings privately, leased them back to WeWork, and collected millions in rent. He even trademarked the word 'We' and sold the trademark rights back to his own company for nearly six million dollars in stock.

Investors panicked. In just six weeks, WeWork's valuation crashed from forty-seven billion dollars down to ten billion. The IPO had to be canceled.

In mid-September, the board ousted Neumann as CEO. But he didn't leave empty-handed. SoftBank handed him a one-point-seven billion dollar exit package — including two hundred and forty-five million in upfront cash and one hundred and eighty-five million in consulting fees.

The man who drove a company into the ground, lost forty billion dollars in valuation, and once burned office furniture at a company party was handed a fortune for his failure.

In October 2019, SoftBank was forced to bail out WeWork with an emergency nine-point-five billion dollar rescue package. Masayoshi Son later publicly admitted that his faith in Neumann had been a humiliating error.

## [13:00 – 14:30] — ACT FIVE: THE BANKRUPTCY — THE INEVITABLE END

WeWork tried to survive under new management. In 2021, it merged with a SPAC at a discounted nine-billion-dollar valuation. But the structural wounds were fatal.

When the pandemic popularized remote work and office vacancies surged, WeWork's lease commitments became an inescapable trap. By 2023, the stock was down ninety-eight percent, and the company was worth less than forty-five million dollars — while its debt exceeded eighteen billion dollars.

On November 6th, 2023, WeWork officially filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

The company listed fifteen billion dollars in assets against eighteen point six billion dollars in liabilities. Thousands of employees lost their jobs, seeing their stock options wiped out. Hundreds of landlords were left holding billions in unpaid rent. The dream was officially dead.

## [14:30 – 15:00] — CLOSING: THE MAN WHO STAYED A BILLIONAIRE

So what happened to Adam Neumann?

He was never charged with a crime. As of 2026, his net worth is estimated at two point eight billion dollars. He still lives in luxury across Manhattan and the Hamptons. And in proof that venture capital never learns, Neumann launched a new real estate startup called Flow — raising three hundred and fifty million dollars from Andreessen Horowitz before even launching.

The WeWork saga is the definitive story of mass delusion in modern capitalism. A story of how charisma, alcohol, and empty promises convinced the smartest investors in the world to hand over billions of dollars.

Masayoshi Son poured sixteen billion dollars into WeWork and lost most of it, alongside his reputation. Neumann walked away with a fortune. Because in modern startup finance, it doesn't matter how much you lose. What matters is how much you take with you when you fall.
