# The Man Who Outprinted the Bank of England — Raw Source Script

Picture this: a twenty-pound note lands on the felt of a London casino table. It's counted. It's stacked. By morning, it's inside the vault of the Bank of England itself — verified, stamped, genuine. Except it isn't. It never was. It was born days earlier in a garden shed in Essex, on a secondhand printing press, in the hands of a man with ink under his fingernails and no criminal record.

For four years, that shed produced what Scotland Yard would later call the largest counterfeiting operation in British history — tens of millions of pounds so convincing that the Bank of England's own machines couldn't catch it. This is the story of how two very different men nearly broke a three-hundred-year-old institution, and how the smallest, dumbest mistake brought the whole thing down.

It starts with a hustler and a craftsman.

Steven Jory grew up in East London selling knockoffs — fake Chanel No. 5 poured into bottles, printed labels slapped on in a back room, sold on street markets until Customs and Excise shut him down. He wasn't dangerous. He was ambitious. And by the early nineties, he was dating the daughter of a man named Kenneth Mainstone.

Mainstone ran a small commercial print shop in Essex. Business cards, leaflets, wedding invitations — nothing you'd look at twice. He'd spent over twenty years learning paper chemistry, color separation, offset printing. He was quiet. Respectable. The kind of man neighbors describe as "always says hello."

Jory saw something else in him: a skill set that, aimed at the right target, could be worth millions.

It started over Sunday dinners. Casual conversation. A joke, maybe, about how good Mainstone's printing really was. Then it stopped being a joke. Mainstone moved his commercial press into an outbuilding behind his own house, and a law-abiding family man became the technical brain behind one of the most sophisticated counterfeiting rings Britain had ever seen.

Their target: the Bank of England's Series D twenty-pound note, featuring the scientist Michael Faraday — at the time, one of the most heavily defended pieces of paper on Earth.

Four layers stood in their way. Custom-blended cotton-linen paper that stayed dark under UV light. Intaglio printing — raised, textured ink pressed in by heavy metal plates, the kind you could feel with your thumb. A watermark of Faraday's face, woven into the paper's thickness during manufacturing, not printed onto it. And a metallic security thread, embedded inside the wet paper fibers before the sheet ever existed.

The Bank's logic was simple: to fake this note, you'd need to be a paper mill and a mint, working together.

Mainstone didn't back down. He tracked down specialty rag paper that behaved exactly like the real thing under UV light. He broke the note down into seventeen separate color plates by hand, rebuilding Faraday's portrait layer by layer on his own press.

The metallic thread was the real problem. You can't weave a thread into paper that's already been made. So his team improvised, printing each note in two passes and bonding a thin foil strip into the surface while the ink was still curing, then overprinting it with the tell-tale broken white dashes. Held up to the light, it looked identical to the real security thread. A bank, a mill, and a mint — all beaten from a garden shed.

Mainstone was obsessive about it, too. For every note that left that shed, two or three imperfect sheets went straight into the fire out back. Nothing sloppy made it out the door.

While Mainstone perfected the product, Jory built the pipeline. By 1996, fake twenties — and soon fifties — were moving through bookmakers, casinos, pubs, and petrol stations across South London, sold in bulk to middlemen who fed them straight into the cash economy.

At the operation's peak, investigators believe two out of every three counterfeit notes in circulation across the UK came out of that one shed — somewhere around fifty million pounds in face value. And here's the part that should terrify you: the Bank of England's own automated sorting machines, the ones meant to catch fakes before they went back into circulation, were stamping them genuine.

The situation got so bad that the Bank seriously debated pulling the entire Faraday note out of circulation — a decision that would have cost tens of millions of pounds and been a very public admission that their currency had failed. Instead, they launched a covert investigation, codenamed Operation Mermaid, to hunt down the source before anyone found out how close the system had come to breaking.

Scotland Yard quickly realized chasing the cash itself was pointless — it moved like water, no names attached, no trail to follow. So they pivoted to the supply chain. Someone in Britain was buying very specific things: non-brightened paper, specialty inks, hot-foil stamping equipment. Jory's crew had been careful — fake names, front addresses, cash wherever possible.

But one supplier made a routine mistake. They added the buyer's name to a marketing mailing list. Months after the operation moved locations, glossy trade catalogs kept arriving at the old, abandoned warehouse address.

New tenants moved in, found a pile of junk mail addressed to nobody they knew, and threw it straight in the trash. A Scotland Yard officer, working through commercial waste as part of the investigation, pulled one of those catalogs back out.

On the label: a customer reference number. Traced back through the supplier's records, it led to a single credit card payment, made before the gang switched entirely to cash. Around the same time, investigators recovered a bundle of counterfeit notes with something tucked inside: a promotional flyer for Mainstone's print shop, complete with a handwritten pager number for Steven Jory.

That was it. That was the whole case.

On August 14th, 1998, the raids came all at once. Police hit an associate's base on the Isle of Wight and recovered one and a half million pounds in uncut sheets. At the same time, they moved on the garden shed in Essex, finding the press, the plates, and Mainstone's hand-cut color separations still sitting there.

Jory and most of his network pleaded guilty and cooperated. Mainstone fought it, insisting in court that he genuinely believed his press was only being used to print glossy magazines — nothing illegal. The jury didn't believe a word of it. As the technical mind behind the entire operation, he was sentenced to twelve years — four years more than the man who'd recruited him in the first place.

The Bank of England was forced to rush out a completely redesigned note, at a cost of more than one hundred million pounds to the British economy.

Two men in a garden shed had done what almost nobody thought was possible: they'd made a three-hundred-year-old central bank rewrite its own money. And in the end, the man who'd spent a lifetime mastering every kind of paper was finally caught by the one piece of paper he never touched — a piece of junk mail nobody was even supposed to read.
